\begin{abstract}
While surveillance camera networks offer high-coverage visual feeds, few benchmarks jointly evaluate camera-level vehicle perception and network-wide forecasting under motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic. The contribution of this paper is a systematic analysis of how visual perception error propagates into multi-horizon graph forecasting under simulated sensing noise; the engineering application is a camera-only, two-stage perception-and-forecasting pipeline evaluated on IC4SD-Traffic-HCM, a released 608-node camera-sensed traffic benchmark for Ho Chi Minh City. Stage 1 uses EfficientNet-B5 for direct category-specific vehicle-count regression. Stage 2 evaluates seven established spatio-temporal graph and Transformer architectures alongside Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (TA-STGCN), a reference adaptation of the Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (STGCN) that inserts temporal self-attention between its convolutional blocks. On clean inputs the models are practically equivalent (TA-STGCN Mean Absolute Error $= 3.2401 \pm 0.0108$, $\sim$0.9\% above STGCN, Cohen's $d = -0.032$); we do not claim a clean-input advance. Under simulated camera noise, robustness stratifies by competitor class: TA-STGCN substantially outperforms non-attention graph and recurrent baselines (Cohen's $d$ up to $+5.84$ vs.\ GraphWaveNet at the highest noise level) but shows only a small-to-medium margin over attention-based baselines under extreme noise (STAEformer, iTransformer; $d \in [0.59, 0.67]$ at Level 4). We conclude that attention-based temporal modeling is more robust to perception noise on this benchmark, with TA-STGCN offering a parameter-economical realization of that robustness (453K parameters, 25.2\% less peak training memory than STAEformer) rather than a dominant one. A prospective edge-cloud architecture transmitting counts rather than images offers privacy-preserving, bandwidth-efficient deployment, pending device-level validation of latency, energy use, and privacy guarantees.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rapid urbanization and the surging influx of personal vehicles have turned urban mobility management into a paramount challenge for metropolitan centers worldwide. Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) address this through applications such as adaptive traffic signal control \citep{signal_control}, dynamic route guidance, and proactive congestion warning. These applications require two fundamental capabilities: accurate real-time monitoring of current traffic states (\emph{traffic perception}) and reliable multi-horizon prediction across the urban grid (\emph{traffic flow forecasting}). Proactive 15-to-30-minute lookahead forecasting in particular enables traffic management centers to adjust signal timing plans and dispatch responders to early-stage bottleneck corridors before severe gridlock materializes.

However, deploying traditional traffic monitoring infrastructure---such as physical induction loop detectors or overhead radar sensors---across entire city road networks incurs prohibitively high installation, road-trenching, and hardware maintenance costs. In fast-growing Southeast Asian metropolises like Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, this challenge is compounded by motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic, where dense, unstructured vehicle streams break strict lane boundaries and render induction loops physically unviable. In contrast, existing municipal Closed-Circuit Television (CCTV) surveillance camera networks offer high-coverage, cost-effective visual feeds at scale.


Camera-based sensing, however, does not by itself deliver network-wide forecasts, and the spatio-temporal forecasting literature offers limited guidance for this setting: its benchmarks and architectures were built around a sensing regime that camera networks do not share \citep{dcrnn, stgcn}, and around lane-disciplined flow that motorcycle-dominant traffic does not exhibit \citep{cao2007, lee2009}. More consequentially, camera-derived counts are \emph{estimates} rather than measurements---they carry systematic error that grows precisely where forecasting matters most, under heavy occlusion at congested intersections. How that perception error propagates through a network-wide forecasting model is, to our knowledge, not quantified in the existing literature, and no public benchmark couples camera-only sensing with mixed traffic at city scale.

Beyond the sensing gap, the task itself is not a single perception problem. A camera observes only its own intersection, whereas traffic management requires forecasts over the connected road network: congestion at one intersection propagates to downstream links, so forecasting each camera as an independent time series discards the spatial structure that governs how congestion spreads \citep{ctm}. We therefore adopt a two-stage formulation: (1) \textbf{camera-level perception}, estimating category-specific vehicle counts (passenger cars and motorcycles) from individual static snapshots, and (2) \textbf{network-wide forecasting}, predicting multi-step traffic volume across the interconnected road graph. Decoupling the two stages keeps the perception model lightweight enough to run per camera and lets the forecasting model operate on compact numerical counts rather than imagery; the cost is that forecasting inherits whatever error the perception stage makes.

For Stage 1 we benchmark five visual backbones under a shared direct-regression head, since dense motorcycle occlusion makes per-vehicle detection and class-agnostic density estimation ill-suited to this setting (Section~\ref{sec:related}). For Stage 2 we adapt the efficient STGCN \citep{stgcn} into \textbf{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network) by inserting a Temporal Self-Attention module \citep{attention} between its two ST-Conv blocks, evaluated empirically as a way to reweight temporal context in the presence of two-stage perception errors.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized below:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A city-scale camera-only benchmark for mixed traffic.} We release \emph{IC4SD-Traffic-HCM}, a 608-node traffic forecasting benchmark derived entirely from municipal camera infrastructure in Ho Chi Minh City, pairing manually annotated vehicle-count imagery with the corresponding city-wide multi-horizon time series, topological road distances, and RBF-weighted adjacency matrices. To our knowledge this is the first openly described camera-sensed, category-preserving benchmark at this scale for a motorcycle-dominant metropolis, and it is intended as a shared testbed on which future architectures can be compared under matched camera-sensed conditions.
    \item \textbf{A two-stage camera-sensed pipeline.} We design and evaluate a framework bridging camera-level visual perception with network-wide spatio-temporal graph forecasting, benchmarking five visual backbones for category-specific vehicle counting under severe occlusion.
    \item \textbf{An end-to-end analysis of two-stage error propagation.} We benchmark seven representative graph and Transformer baselines plus a reference adaptation (TA-STGCN) across five random seeds, characterizing how Stage 1 perception error cascades into Stage 2 forecasting under point accuracy, injected sensing noise, seed variance, and hardware cost. Given the evaluation sample size, we report effect sizes (Cohen's $d$) rather than $p$-values.
    \item \textbf{TA-STGCN as a design point.} Inserting temporal self-attention into STGCN is practically free on clean inputs yet converts into a large robustness gain under camera-sensed noise (Section~\ref{sec:noise_robustness}), illustrating the robustness--cost trade-off the benchmark is designed to expose.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the datasets, the two-stage methodology, and the experimental configuration. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations} discusses the implications, hardware efficiency, and deployment limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Building city-scale traffic intelligence from camera networks requires progress on two coupled fronts: extracting reliable vehicle measurements from visual feeds, and forecasting their spatio-temporal evolution. Most existing literature addresses these problems under conditions that differ sharply from the camera-sensed, motorcycle-dominant urban traffic this study targets. This section reviews both fronts---visual counting under dense occlusion, and spatio-temporal forecasting architectures including the hybrid and adaptive-graph designs that bridge efficiency and accuracy---and then characterizes the gap that remains for mixed, motorcycle-dominant traffic sensed entirely by camera.

\subsection{Visual Vehicle Counting Under Dense Traffic}
\label{sec:related_counting}
Visual vehicle counting traditionally relies on Tracking-by-Detection pipelines (e.g., YOLO + ByteTrack \citep{yolo}), density-map estimation (e.g., MCNN \citep{mcnn}, CSRNet \citep{csrnet}, CAN \citep{can}), or point-supervised counters such as P2PNet and DM-Count. The latter two families are effective in dense scenes but require point-level or density-map ground truth and per-camera calibration---substantially more expensive than the aggregate count labels used here, since each image must be annotated with per-vehicle dots or a per-pixel density map and each viewpoint re-calibrated, multiplying cost across hundreds of nodes. The prevailing alternatives are ill-suited for the targeted deployment for three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Mutual Occlusion and NMS Computational Bottlenecks:} In developing nations, motorcycles do not adhere to strict lane discipline, resulting in severe physical occlusion during congestion (in our dataset, dense clusters frequently exhibit 80--90\% of vehicles partially or fully occluded in a single frame). Bounding boxes overlap catastrophically, generating thousands of candidate boxes ($M$); classical Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) incurs $\mathcal{O}(M^2)$ pairwise Intersection-over-Union complexity \citep{softnms}, turning post-processing into a severe latency bottleneck on edge devices while failing to resolve dense motorcycle clusters.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Discontinuity:} Public municipal cameras typically provide static image snapshots at low polling frequencies (e.g., 1 frame per minute) to conserve bandwidth. This severe temporal sparsity breaks the continuous-frame assumption required for Tracking-by-Detection algorithms to function.
    \item \textbf{Edge Hardware Constraints:} Running heavy multi-object tracking pipelines per camera node is economically prohibitive for city-scale deployments. Density maps are computationally lighter but inherently class-agnostic, destroying the vital car-vs-motorcycle distinction.
\end{enumerate}

To bypass these limitations, we adopt a \textbf{Direct Regression} approach, mapping static camera images directly to continuous, category-preserving vehicle counts: $I \to [\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. While direct regression can overfit global background contexts (e.g., lighting or road geometry), our qualitative feature-attribution analysis (via Grad-CAM++ in Section~\ref{sec:results}) indicates that the selected backbone concentrates activations on active vehicle clusters. We position direct regression as a practical choice for this dataset rather than a universal replacement for specialized counting methods; a direct comparison is left to future work when matched point annotations and camera calibration are available.

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Traffic Flow Forecasting}
\label{sec:related_forecasting}
Traffic forecasting has evolved from traditional statistical baselines (e.g., ARIMA \citep{arima}, SVR \citep{svr}) to sequential RNNs (e.g., DCRNN \citep{dcrnn}, T-GCN \citep{tgcn}, GC-LSTM \citep{gclstm}), spectral GCNs (e.g., Kipf \& Welling \citep{gcn}), and modern STGNN architectures (e.g., STGCN \citep{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \citep{wavenet}, STID \citep{stid}, TPA-LSTM \citep{tpalstm}, ST-GNCDE \citep{stgncde}); detailed taxonomies are provided in comprehensive surveys \citep{yin_survey, gnn_survey}. While convolutional models are highly efficient, their localized 1D temporal receptive fields struggle with non-stationary long-range dependencies. Conversely, pure self-attention Transformers (e.g., ASTGCN \citep{astgcn}, STAEformer \citep{staeformer}) achieve high accuracy but incur prohibitive quadratic spatial-temporal memory footprints across large urban graphs. We benchmark against a representative set spanning both paradigms, detailed in Section~\ref{sec:baselines_config}.

\subsection{Hybrid Architectures and Adaptive Graphs}
\label{sec:related_hybrid}
To bridge the gap between convolutional efficiency and transformer accuracy, recent hybrid architectures explore mixing CNNs and Transformers (e.g., PDFormer \citep{pdformer}). Additionally, recognizing the limitations of static distance-based graphs, modern STGNNs increasingly utilize dynamic or adaptive adjacency matrices to capture shifting correlations (e.g., STAG-GCN \citep{staggcn}, STJGCN \citep{stjgcn}), though these methods often remain computationally expensive. TA-STGCN belongs to this hybrid family, decoupling spatial Chebyshev convolutions from temporal self-attention, coupling the efficiency of convolutional ST-Conv blocks with a global temporal receptive field.

Prevailing architectures are developed on highly structured, car-dominant highway benchmarks (e.g., METR-LA, PEMS-BAY) collected via induction loop detectors \citep{dcrnn, stgcn}, which assume predictable fluid-dynamic flows and strict lane discipline. In contrast, urban traffic in Southeast Asian metropolises is chaotic and motorcycle-dominant: as established in classical and modern traffic studies \citep{cao2007, lee2009, mixedtraffic_stgnn}, vehicles cluster densely without lane adherence, causing highly non-stationary temporal fluctuations and sudden spatial spillovers. Because camera-sensed counts are estimates rather than direct measurements, they carry perception error that is largest precisely under severe occlusion; how that error propagates through a multi-step network-wide forecast is an open question this study addresses empirically.

Camera-based pipelines have recently begun to address this gap \citep{feng2024, liu2024}, yet they primarily target structured, car-dominant environments; similarly, localized camera-based studies in Southeast Asian metropolises such as Hanoi \citep{vu2026} focus on isolated intersections rather than continuous, city-scale graph forecasting.


As Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison} makes explicit, no existing benchmark addresses the combined challenge of camera-only sensing and chaotic mixed traffic at a network-wide scale, and none preserves per-category vehicle information beyond the perception stage. This study is positioned directly at that intersection, coupling category-preserving direct-regression perception with network-wide graph forecasting on a real, city-scale 608-node camera network in Ho Chi Minh City.

\subsection{IC4SD-Traffic-HCM Dataset and Graph Construction}
\label{sec:datasets}

\subsubsection{Camera Network Acquisition}

Traffic camera imagery and station metadata were collected from the public Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal, which provides open civic access to surveillance feeds for transportation monitoring and non-commercial research. An automated client retrieves the portal's public camera registry, from which we extract the identifier, location name, and geographical coordinates (latitude, longitude) of each station; these coordinates define the spatial positions of the graph nodes. All retrieved material consists of publicly accessible still snapshots, processed solely for non-commercial academic research; no personally identifiable information is recorded or stored.

For continuous observation, a multi-threaded downloader with HTTP session pooling queries the image endpoints of all 657 registered cameras on a fixed 60-second cycle, keeping samples across the network temporally aligned. Retrieved images are written to a time-indexed directory structure for batch processing. Cameras with transient connection failures, ``Image not available'' payloads, or persistent downtime were excluded, leaving \textbf{608 active stations} as the final node set $\mathcal{V}$ ($N = |\mathcal{V}| = 608$); acquisition continued on the same 60-second cycle over this final set for the remainder of the collection period. Figure~\ref{fig:api_samples} shows representative snapshots from the resulting collection. The samples illustrate the operating conditions the perception stage must handle: oblique and varying camera viewpoints, day and night illumination, and dense motorcycle clusters with extensive mutual occlusion.

\subsubsection{IC4SD-Traffic-HCM-Vision Dataset}

The vision component of the benchmark comprises \textbf{5,012 manually annotated traffic images} drawn from the camera network described above. Each image is labelled with two continuous targets: the number of passenger cars and the number of motorcycles present in the frame. Images are resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels and normalized with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation prior to training.

The annotations contain an average of 30.28 vehicles per image (SD = 25.07), with a median of 24.00 and a range of 0--194 vehicles. Motorcycles dominate the scenes (mean: 23.02, max: 178) relative to passenger cars (mean: 7.26, max: 50), producing a right-skewed distribution that includes highly congested cases. Although acquisition runs continuously over the full 24-hour cycle, the annotated subset was sampled from selected diurnal windows to cover distinct operational regimes at manageable labelling cost (Table~\ref{tab:time_distribution}).

All 5,012 images were annotated independently by three trained annotators following a common counting protocol, with each annotator labelling the complete set rather than a disjoint partition. Discrepancies were then reconciled through cross-checking: images on which the annotators disagreed beyond a preset tolerance were jointly reviewed and re-counted to produce the final ground-truth labels. To quantify labelling consistency before reconciliation, we computed the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient ($\text{ICC}(3,k)$, two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement) on a random subset of 500 images (approximately 10\% of the dataset): $\text{ICC}=0.984$ for passenger cars and $\text{ICC}=0.962$ for motorcycles (combined $\text{ICC}=0.973$, $p<0.01$). Agreement remained above $0.99$ under low-density conditions but decreased to $\text{ICC}=0.941$ for motorcycles under extreme congestion and severe partial occlusion, reflecting the intrinsic visual ambiguity of dense motorcycle clusters.

Because the supervision signal is an image-level count, augmentation is restricted to label-preserving transformations: horizontal flipping ($p=0.5$), photometric colour jittering (brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue, each $\pm 15\%$), and small-scale random erasing ($p=0.2$, scale $2\%$--$20\%$) to simulate localized occlusion without altering the true count. Mini-batch stratification across diurnal windows is applied to limit temporal exposure bias. Spatially targeted strategies such as dense-region cropping or synthetic congestion synthesis would require point-level annotations and are left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations}).

One limitation of the dataset should be stated directly: the annotated set is small relative to the visual diversity of 608 camera perspectives, backgrounds, and lighting conditions, with three consequences for the results reported later. First, data-hungry backbones such as the $\sim$86M-parameter ViT-Base underperform and show higher seed variability in this regime (Table~\ref{tab:stage1_seed_variance}); this should not be read as a general verdict on Transformer backbones. Second, because Stage 2 trains on Stage 1 pseudo-labels, Stage 1 generalization directly bounds the forecasting fidelity of the full pipeline. Third, the spatially disjoint evaluation split (Section~\ref{sec:data_partitioning}) estimates generalization to unseen camera perspectives, but its size limits fine-grained per-camera or per-density claims.


\subsubsection{IC4SD-Traffic-HCM-Graph Dataset}

The Stage 2 time-series dataset is generated by deploying the optimal Stage 1 perception backbone, EfficientNet-B5 (selected based on Pareto-optimal accuracy, FLOPs efficiency, and seed consistency as established in Section~\ref{sec:subproblem1_results}), to perform continuous inference over 1-minute camera snapshots across all 608 active graph nodes throughout the 86-day observation period (June 23, 2025, to September 16, 2025). The resulting 1-minute series is smoothed and decimated to 5-minute time steps ($\Delta t = 5$ min) following the causal procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:data_partitioning}.

The resulting city-wide time-series matrix encompasses exactly \textbf{24,768 timesteps} across $N=608$ spatial nodes for $2$ vehicle categories (passenger cars and motorcycles), forming a primary dataset tensor of shape $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{total}} \in \mathbb{R}^{24768 \times 608 \times 2}$. At this stage the series contains only the two category-specific vehicle-count channels produced by Stage 1; the three deterministic temporal covariates used as additional Stage 2 inputs are introduced during historical feature construction in Section~\ref{sec:historical_features}, yielding $F=5$ input features per node and time step.


\subsubsection{Road Graph Construction and Chebyshev Preprocessing}

To capture spatial traffic spillover across the city, estimated vehicle counts across the 608 camera locations are integrated into an urban road network graph, modeled as a directed weighted spatial graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$, where $\mathcal{V}$ is the set of camera nodes ($N=608$), $\mathcal{E}$ the directed road connections, and $W$ a Radial Basis Function (RBF) weighted distance matrix.

Graph edges $\mathcal{E}$ represent physical directed road connections between adjacent camera stations ($2,452$ valid directed edges). To reflect real-world urban road topology---where physical barriers (such as the Saigon River, expressways, and flyovers) or one-way traffic restrictions prevent unconstrained spatial spillover---directed edges $(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}$ are established strictly along valid physical traffic flow corridors derived from OpenStreetMap (OSM) network routing topology. Specifically, a directed edge is only created if there is a direct route from node $i$ to node $j$ without traversing any other intermediate camera node.

For any valid directed connection $(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}$, edge weight $W_{ij} \in [0, 1]$ is computed using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel based on $d_{ij}$, which denotes the shortest-path network routing distance along valid physical road segments between node $i$ and node $j$ (in kilometers):
\begin{equation}
W_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
\exp\left(
-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{\sigma_{\mathrm{RBF}}^2}
\right),
& \text{if } (i,j) \in \mathcal{E} \text{ and } d_{ij} \le \tau, \\
0,
& \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\mathrm{RBF}} = 1.0887$ km is the empirical standard deviation of the valid directed road-network distances and $\tau = 3.3945$ km is the distance cutoff selected when constructing the sparse OSM graph. These values are descriptive graph-construction settings, not learned model parameters; we did not conduct a separate sensitivity study. If no physical road link connects node $i$ to node $j$, or if one-way traffic constraints prohibit direct movement from node $i$ to node $j$, $W_{ij} = 0$, ensuring that spatial graph convolutions propagate feature representations strictly along physically permissible urban flow paths.

Recent spatio-temporal graph models such as GraphWaveNet \citep{wavenet} and DSTAGNN \citep{dstagnn} use adaptive graph learning to infer spatial dependencies from node features. Although a static graph cannot capture all functional relationships between distant locations, we retain this static OSM-derived RBF adjacency matrix as a constrained and interpretable alternative for two practical reasons. First, Stage 2 inputs inherit Stage 1 perception noise; restricting message passing to physically valid road connections and the distance cutoff limits spurious long-range correlations between disconnected roads. Second, avoiding dense adaptive $N \times N$ adjacency maps reduces the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ memory overhead incurred by methods that materialize such maps, which is relevant for the $N=608$ network and the deployment-oriented efficiency comparison in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}. This design choice is therefore evaluated as a practical physical prior rather than claimed to be universally superior to adaptive graph learning.

To prepare the adjacency matrix for spatial feature propagation, we apply two-sided degree normalization directly to the directed matrix $W$, preserving one-way corridor structure:
\begin{equation}
L = I_N - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}, \quad \tilde{L} = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} L - I_N,
\end{equation}
where $D_{ii} = \sum_{j} W_{ij}$ is the diagonal out-degree matrix and $\lambda_{\max}$ rescales the operator prior to the order-$K$ Chebyshev expansion, which propagates node features over $K$-hop directed neighbourhoods without explicit eigendecomposition.


\subsubsection{Data Preprocessing, Partitioning, and Leakage Control}
\label{sec:data_partitioning}

The Stage 2 preprocessing and evaluation pipeline adheres strictly to a sequential workflow that preserves temporal causality and eliminates data leakage.

Raw 1-minute camera inference records are first aligned onto a uniform 1-minute temporal grid. During offline dataset curation, transient API transmission dropouts spanning $\le 30$ minutes are imputed using bounded linear temporal interpolation between flanking valid observations to reconstruct continuous count series, consistent with standard traffic benchmark curation protocols (e.g., PeMS, METR-LA). While bidirectional interpolation utilizes the subsequent valid observation within small dropout gaps during offline curation, it is restricted entirely to the offline preparation stage prior to chronological partitioning. We further verified that no imputed dropout gap spans a split boundary, so no interpolated value within one split is derived from observations belonging to another. All subsequent temporal regularizations are strictly causal: high-frequency perception noise is smoothed using a causal double-rolling filter comprising a 3-minute backward rolling mean ($window_1=3, \text{center}=\text{False}$) followed by a forward-in-time exponential moving average ($\text{span}=5$). The filtered series is then decimated to the target 5-minute sampling interval ($\Delta t = 5$ min), yielding the complete 24,768-step matrix. Because camera snapshots capture instantaneous vehicle presence rather than cumulative flow, low-pass filtering followed by decimation is used instead of block aggregation such as summation.

The continuous 24,768-step sequence is partitioned chronologically into Training (first 19,814 timesteps; 80\%), Validation (next 2,477 timesteps; 10\%), and Testing (final 2,477 timesteps; 10\%) splits, with corresponding tensors $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{train}} \in \mathbb{R}^{19814 \times 608 \times 2}$, $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{val}} \in \mathbb{R}^{2477 \times 608 \times 2}$, and $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{test}} \in \mathbb{R}^{2477 \times 608 \times 2}$. Applying a sliding window with historical lookback $T_{\text{in}}=24$ (120 minutes) and forecast horizon $H=6$ (30 minutes) yields 19,785 Training samples, 2,472 Validation samples, and 2,472 Test samples. Validation and Test forecast targets remain strictly within their respective chronological splits, while the earliest historical lookbacks may extend into the immediately preceding split using only observations that precede the corresponding forecast origin. Thus, the window construction itself does not introduce future-target leakage. The resulting Stage 2 input tensor shapes are specified after the $F=5$ node feature representation is defined in Section~\ref{sec:historical_features}.

To reduce scale bias across heterogeneous camera locations without inducing distributional look-ahead leakage, vehicle-count time series are standardized per node:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{v}_{i,t}
=
\frac{v_{i,t} - \mu_i}
{\sigma_i + \epsilon_{\mathrm{norm}}},
\quad
\epsilon_{\mathrm{norm}} = 10^{-6},
\end{equation}
where mean $\mu_i$ and standard deviation $\sigma_i$ are computed strictly from the Training split (first 80\%) and subsequently applied frozen to the Validation and Test splits.

Finally, the two stages use complementary isolation schemes. For Stage 1, spatial isolation applies: the five vision backbones are trained only on the 4,010 images from Stage 1 Training camera locations, while the 501 Validation images and 501 Test images come from camera stations at intersections disjoint from the Training locations, so no Stage 1 Validation or Test images are used to optimize the perception models. For Stage 2, temporal isolation applies: model parameters are optimized only on the first 80\% chronological Training interval, the subsequent Validation and Test intervals are excluded from the training loss, and the final 10\% Test interval is reserved for forecasting evaluation, with no Validation or Test target values entering Stage 2 parameter optimization.


\subsection{Stage 1: Vehicle Counting}
\label{sec:subproblem1}

Stage 1 maps each camera image to continuous category-specific vehicle counts $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. We benchmark five visual backbones for motorcycle-dominant traffic scenes: ResNet-50 \citep{resnet}, EfficientNet-B5 \citep{efficientnet}, ConvNeXt-Tiny \citep{convnext}, MobileNetV3-Large \citep{mobilenet}, and ViT-Base/16 \citep{vit}.

\subsubsection{Unified MLP Regression Head}

For each backbone, a pooled feature vector $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is extracted using global average pooling for the four convolutional backbones and the \texttt{[CLS]} token for ViT-Base/16. The feature dimension is $d=2048$ for ResNet-50 and EfficientNet-B5, $d=960$ for MobileNetV3-Large, and $d=768$ for ConvNeXt-Tiny and ViT-Base/16. The original ImageNet classification layer is replaced by the same MLP regression head for all backbones:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_1 = \text{LayerNorm}\left(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{b}_1\right),
\quad \mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times d},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
=
\mathbf{W}_2\,\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{h}_1)+\mathbf{b}_2,
\quad \mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 128},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{c}}=[\hat{c}_{\text{car}},\hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$. Using a common regression head keeps the task-specific component fixed across backbones, allowing their visual representations to be compared under the same prediction head.

\subsubsection{Objective Function}

Stage 1 is trained end-to-end using the Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) loss \citep{huber}, averaged over the passenger-car and motorcycle residuals. For residual $x=c_m-\hat{c}_m$, $m\in\{\text{car},\text{bike}\}$, and threshold $\delta=1$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Huber}}(x)=
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{2}x^2, & |x|<\delta,\\
\delta\left(|x|-\frac{1}{2}\delta\right), & |x|\ge\delta.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The quadratic regime provides smooth optimization for small residuals, while the linear regime reduces the influence of large errors arising from severe occlusion, annotation ambiguity, and long-tailed vehicle counts. This makes Huber loss suitable for the noisy count-regression setting used in both stages of the pipeline.


\subsection{Stage 2: TA-STGCN Forecasting}
\label{sec:graph_tastgcn}

\subsubsection{Historical Feature Tensor Construction}
\label{sec:historical_features}

Stage 2 receives sliding windows of historical node features at a temporal resolution of $\Delta t=5$ minutes. This resolution reduces short-term perception jitter while retaining macroscopic traffic dynamics and lowers temporal storage and tensor-processing overhead relative to modeling the full 1-minute sequence.

At each time step, the two category-specific vehicle-count channels generated by Stage 1 are combined with three deterministic temporal covariates, giving $F=5$ features per node:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Passenger car count $c_{\text{car}}^{(i,t)}$.
    \item Motorcycle count $c_{\text{bike}}^{(i,t)}$.
    \item Sine time-of-day feature: $\sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Cosine time-of-day feature: $\cos\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Normalized hour of day: $\frac{\text{hour}}{24}$.
\end{enumerate}

The historical feature tensor at forecast origin $t$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X}^{(t)}
=
\left[
X_{t-T_{\text{in}}+1},
X_{t-T_{\text{in}}+2},
\dots,
X_t
\right]
\in
\mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{in}} \times N \times F},
\end{equation}
where $N=608$, $T_{\text{in}}=24$ corresponds to 120 minutes of history, and $F=5$. The sliding-window construction in Section~\ref{sec:data_partitioning} therefore yields input tensors of shape $[19785 \times 24 \times 608 \times 5]$ for Training and $[2472 \times 24 \times 608 \times 5]$ for Validation and Test. In batched form, $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{in}} \times N \times F}$, and the prediction target is the next $H=6$ category-specific vehicle-count steps, $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 2}$.

\subsubsection{TA-STGCN Architecture}

The overall architecture is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ta_stgcn_architecture}: two stacked ST-Conv blocks with a Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention module inserted between them, followed by a temporal projection that maps the resulting representation to the multi-horizon forecast.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/ta_stgcn_architecture.pdf}
\caption{Architectural overview of TA-STGCN. The pipeline processes $F=5$ input features through two ST-Conv blocks, each coupling 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU, $K_t=3$) with Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions ($K=3$) at $C=80$ hidden channels, with a Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention module ($h=4$) inserted between them. A final 1D temporal projection maps the representation to the $H=6$ category-specific forecast steps.}
\label{fig:ta_stgcn_architecture}
\end{figure}

TA-STGCN extends the standard Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (STGCN) \citep{stgcn} by inserting a {Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention} module \citep{attention} between two ST-Conv blocks. Each ST-Conv block sandwiches a Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolution ($K=3$) \citep{chebnet} between two 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU; kernel size $K_t=3$), followed by LayerNorm and dropout ($p=0.25$) with a residual connection, using $C=80$ hidden channels. The static OSM-RBF graph remains the spatial prior, while temporal attention operates on the post-ST-Conv representation over the full $T_{\text{in}}=24$ historical context. The output layer applies a 1D convolution that collapses the temporal dimension ($T_{\text{in}} \to 1$) and projects the $C=80$ hidden channels onto the $H \times 2$ forecast targets.

The contribution is therefore not a new attention operator---temporal and spatio-temporal attention are well established in models such as ASTGCN \citep{astgcn}, GMAN \citep{gman}, and STAEformer \citep{staeformer}---but a specific \emph{placement and role} of temporal self-attention within a physically constrained STGCN pipeline for camera-sensed traffic. Unlike ASTGCN and GMAN, which attend primarily over the spatial axis (nodes and edges) and only secondarily over time, the module used here reweights exclusively along the temporal axis, independently for each node. By placing it after the first spatio-temporal encoding block and before the second ST-Conv block, TA-STGCN allows broader temporal context to reweight transient fluctuations before final forecasting while retaining OSM-constrained spatial propagation. This design is evaluated directly through the model benchmarks, placement ablation, attention analysis, and noise-robustness experiments in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection{Temporal Self-Attention and Robustness Intuition}

Let $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$ denote the hidden representation produced by the first ST-Conv block. To capture global temporal dependencies without incurring the $\mathcal{O}((N \cdot T_{\text{in}})^2)$ memory cost of joint space--time attention, the module operates along the temporal dimension independently for each spatial node: $\mathbf{H}$ is flattened across batch and node dimensions into $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times T_{\text{in}} \times C}$, so that each node is treated as a length-$T_{\text{in}}$ sequence. For each of the $h=4$ heads (projection dimension $d_k = C/h = 20$), scaled dot-product attention yields a temporal affinity matrix
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}^{(i)} = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_i^{T}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{in}} \times T_{\text{in}}},
\end{equation}
computed from the usual query, key, and value projections of $\mathbf{Z}$. The heads are concatenated and projected back to $C$ channels, and the result passes through two residual Add-and-Norm stages separated by a GELU feed-forward network with $4C$ expansion, following the standard Transformer block \citep{attention}. The head-averaged map $\mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^{h} \mathbf{A}^{(i)}$ is the temporal affinity matrix analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:attention_explainability}.

A simple conditional-variance calculation provides intuition for why temporal reweighting may attenuate transient perturbations. Let $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{s}+\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{in}}}$ denote a single hidden channel at one node, and, conditionally on fixed attention weights $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathbf{A}_{\tau,:} \in \Delta^{T_{\text{in}}-1}$ at query step $\tau$, assume independent zero-mean perturbations with common variance $\sigma_{\eta}^{2}$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attn_var}
\text{Var}\left(
\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}}\alpha_t\eta_t
\right)
=
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}}\alpha_t^2
=
\frac{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}{T_{\text{eff}}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{eff}}=1/\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_2^2\in[1,T_{\text{in}}]$ is the effective attention window. This identity is a stylized motivation rather than a robustness guarantee: the learned attention weights are data-dependent, and Stage 1 errors are neither independent nor homoscedastic. Robustness is therefore established empirically rather than inferred from Eq.~\ref{eq:attn_var}.

The attention-enhanced representation is passed to the second ST-Conv block and the output projection to produce the $H=6$ forecast steps $\hat{Y}$. The network is optimized end-to-end using Huber loss \citep{huber}.

\text{MAE}
=

\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{h=1}^{H}
\sum_{k=1}^{M}
\left|
y_{i,h}^{(k)}-\hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)}
\right|,
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\text{MAPE}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\sum_{(i,h,k)\in\Omega}
\frac{
\left|
y_{i,h}^{(k)}-\hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)}
\right|
}{
y_{i,h}^{(k)}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{MAPE}}
}
\times 100\%,
\end{equation}
where $N=608$, $H=6$, $M$ is the test batch count,
$\Omega=\{(i,h,k)\mid y_{i,h}^{(k)}>0.5\}$ denotes the set of evaluation samples with non-trivial traffic volume, and
$\epsilon_{\mathrm{MAPE}}=10^{-5}$ is a numerical safeguard with negligible effect on the reported values.

\paragraph{Statistical vs. Practical Significance Analysis}
Across the full evaluation sample ($N=608$ nodes, $H=6$ horizons, $M \approx 5{,}000$ test sequence windows, totaling over $1.8 \times 10^7$ prediction points), standard hypothesis tests almost universally yield extreme statistical significance, so we emphasize \emph{effect size} (Cohen's $d$) rather than $p$-values throughout; Section~\ref{sec:variance_analysis} reports the full test statistics.

On unperturbed, clean traffic feeds, the overall point-forecasting improvement of TA-STGCN ($\text{MAE} = 3.2401 \pm 0.0108, \text{MAPE} = 11.32\% \pm 0.04\%$) over GraphWaveNet ($\text{MAE} = 3.2776 \pm 0.0049$) and the STGCN Baseline ($\text{MAE} = 3.2703 \pm 0.0128$) is small in absolute terms ($\approx 0.03$--$0.04$ MAE) with a negligible Cohen's $d$ effect size ($d = -0.024$ and $d = -0.032$, respectively). We therefore do \emph{not} claim a clean-input point-accuracy victory: under ideal, noise-free operational conditions, the leading spatio-temporal graph models are practically equivalent in point-forecasting accuracy. Scaling the STGCN baseline to a larger capacity (STGCN Large, 487K parameters, exceeding TA-STGCN's 453K) does \emph{not} improve accuracy ($\text{MAE} = 3.2805 \pm 0.0134$), confirming that TA-STGCN's residual advantage stems from its temporal-attention inductive bias rather than parameter count.

The practical relevance of TA-STGCN in this benchmark therefore does not rest on the marginal clean-input gain, but on a different and, we argue, deployment-relevant axis. \textbf{TA-STGCN is best read as a robustness-oriented adaptation of its STGCN predecessor:} inserting the temporal self-attention module costs the negligible clean-input effect noted above yet converts into a \emph{large} effect under perception noise. This robustness-vs-cost asymmetry is the core contribution, and it manifests across three operational dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Noise Resilience Relative to the STGCN Predecessor (Primary Claim):} At Level 2 ($\Delta\text{MAE}=4.64$, the mean empirical Stage 1 error), TA-STGCN improves over STGCN Baseline with Cohen's $d = +2.004$; at Level 4 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 13.95$), TA-STGCN obtains $10.1532 \pm 1.1153$ MAE versus $13.1724 \pm 0.3515$ for the STGCN Baseline, $d = +3.651$---the attention insertion trades an essentially free clean-input cost for a structurally large robustness gain over the architecture it adapts. This advantage is not uniform across all competitors but \emph{stratifies by competitor class}; Section~\ref{sec:noise_robustness} reports the full stratification and the resulting design-class reading of the robustness claim.
    \item \textbf{Flatter Long-Horizon Error Accumulation:} At extended 30-minute lookaheads ($t+6$), TA-STGCN bounds error propagation more effectively than GraphWaveNet ($t+6$ MAE $= 3.4947$ vs.\ $3.5800$, a reduction of $0.0853$ MAE and $0.21\%$ MAPE). While this absolute gap is small, it widens monotonically with horizon and compounds across all $608$ nodes and every forecast cycle, which is the regime in which traffic-management decisions (signal-timing and incident-response lead times) are most sensitive to error drift.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Activation Memory Economy:} TA-STGCN reduces peak GPU activation memory by \textbf{25.2\%} compared to STAEformer ($8,606.9$ MB vs.\ $11,509.1$ MB) and trainable parameter count by \textbf{70.4\%} compared to ASTGCN ($453\text{K}$ vs.\ $1,533\text{K}$). It is not the most memory-economical model overall---iTransformer's $265.2$ MB reflects its non-graph inverted design and is treated separately (Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations})---but among graph-structured baselines that materialize an $N\times N$ spatial map, TA-STGCN occupies a favorable accuracy--memory point for cost-constrained municipal deployment.
\end{enumerate}

An omnibus Friedman test across the large evaluation sample (aggregating over multiple horizons and nodes) yields a Chi-Square statistic of $\chi^2 = 9025.15$, and post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Holm-Bonferroni-adjusted) reject equal performance against all baselines ($p < 0.001$). Variance decomposition further reveals that the total forecasting error variance originates from two primary sources: (1) inherent data stochasticity (including visual perception noise from Stage 1 and physical traffic volatility) and (2) model initialization stochasticity. The exceptionally narrow Bootstrap CIs and the minimal Coefficient of Variation ($\text{CV} = 0.33\%$) across independent random seeds demonstrate that TA-STGCN minimizes the initialization variance footprint: its competitiveness is \textbf{not an artifact of lucky initialization stochasticity}, but a consistent architectural property, providing a higher degree of run-to-run consistency than heavy transformer baselines like STAEformer ($\text{CV} = 0.56\%$) without suffering from convergence instability.

Overall, Depth=2 with $C=80$ offers the optimal accuracy-efficiency balance.

\subsection{Robustness Analysis}
\label{sec:noise_robustness}

An inherent vulnerability of two-stage ITS architectures is \emph{two-stage error propagation}: because high-precision inductive loop sensors are absent across city-scale camera networks, Stage 2 graph forecasting models are trained on vehicle count sequences produced by Stage 1 vision perception backbones (which carry intrinsic visual estimation noise).

To rigorously evaluate how Stage 1 perception noise cascades into Stage 2 graph forecasting, we conduct a \textbf{Noise Sensitivity Analysis}. Rather than relying on arbitrary synthetic Gaussian noise, the noise injection parameters and evaluation levels ($\Delta \text{MAE}_{\text{noise}}$) are \textbf{strictly derived from the empirical error statistics of the Stage 1 vision models} evaluated across our 501 test camera feeds.

Specifically, empirical evaluation of Stage 1 backbones (Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}) reveals two fundamental physical characteristics of camera-based vehicle counting under dense mixed traffic:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric Undercounting Bias:} Under heavy congestion, severe inter-vehicle occlusions (motorcycles overlapping passenger cars) consistently bias the visual backbones toward \emph{undercounting} (as reflected by negative prediction bias across dense strata in Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}).
    \item \textbf{Heteroscedastic Scaling:} Absolute counting error magnitudes scale proportionally with local node volume (higher vehicle density inherently incurs larger absolute error bounds).
\end{enumerate}

To capture these empirical properties, we model Stage 1 perception noise using a \emph{Heteroscedastic Asymmetric} Gamma process, where 80\% of injected noise terms are negative (matching the empirical undercounting bias) and the noise scale is proportional to node volume.

We choose the Gamma distribution as the \emph{reference} noise model because it is a positive, right-skewed magnitude law that matches the long-tailed, non-negative count-error magnitudes observed under congestion, and because its two parameters (shape, scale) give a closed-form sampler that we can calibrate exactly to each target $\Delta\text{MAE}$. We do \emph{not} claim it is uniquely correct---Weibull and log-normal alternatives are equally plausible right-skewed choices---and because the injected distribution is an experimenter-controlled modeling assumption rather than a measured ground truth, our robustness conclusions are conditioned on this choice.

Two considerations limit this conditioning in practice. First, the four target $\Delta\text{MAE}$ levels are calibrated to the \emph{empirical} Stage~1 error statistics (minimum, mean, maximum, $+2\sigma_{\mathrm{MAE}}$ stress) rather than to any property specific to the Gamma distribution, so an alternative sampler calibrated to the same targets would probe the same four operating points. Second, the claim attributed to TA-STGCN is an \emph{ordering}---a flatter degradation slope across levels---which depends on the model's response to perturbation severity rather than the exact tail shape of the perturbation. A sensitivity ablation under matched Weibull and log-normal samplers is the correct way to confirm this and remains a priority for future work (Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations}).

The target evaluation levels ($\Delta \text{MAE}_{\text{noise}}$) correspond directly to the empirical perception error distribution generated across individual test cameras in Stage 1:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level 1 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 3.49$):} Grounded in the minimum empirical camera counting error ($\text{MAE}_{\min} = 3.488 \approx 3.49$), representing optimal camera angles and unoccluded off-peak feeds.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 4.64$):} Grounded in the mean empirical perception error ($\text{MAE}_{\text{mean}} = 4.640 \approx 4.64$) averaged across operational traffic camera conditions.
    \item \textbf{Level 3 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 10.25$):} Grounded in the maximum empirical error bound ($\text{MAE}_{\max} = 10.250 \approx 10.25$) recorded at severely occluded, high-density rush-hour intersections (aligning with the large negative motorcycle bias observed under High density in Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}).
    \item \textbf{Level 4 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 13.95$):} A \emph{stress test} that extrapolates beyond the observed empirical error range ($\text{MAE}_{\max} + 2\sigma_{\text{MAE}} = 10.25 + 2 \times 1.849 \approx 13.95$). This level models a hypothetical compound-failure state rather than a directly observed camera condition; it is included solely to probe robustness at the tail of the error distribution and should be interpreted as a stress test, not as a measured operational scenario.
\end{itemize}

The implication for the contribution is direct: the noise analysis is best stated as ``attention-based temporal modeling is substantially more robust to perception noise than non-attention graph/RNN modeling, and TA-STGCN is one attention-equipped architecture that realizes this; among attention-equipped models the differences are small.'' TA-STGCN does not demonstrably \emph{dominate} STAEformer or iTransformer under noise---its robustness is on the same order as theirs---while it remains substantially more robust than the non-attention graph baselines and far more parameter-economical than STAEformer. We therefore frame the noise result as a robustness \emph{property of the attention-equipped design class on this benchmark}, not as a victory of TA-STGCN over the SOTA attention baselines.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:deployment_limitations}

\subsection{Envisioned Edge-Cloud Architecture (Future Work)}
\label{sec:deployment_arch}

To motivate the deployment-oriented choices that the experiments \emph{do} evaluate (parameter economy, memory footprint, and count-only payloads), we briefly outline a prospective edge-cloud split. We stress that this is a \emph{design sketch for future work, not an experimental result}: the pipeline used for all reported results is the centralized one described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}, and none of our experiments measure device-level latency, energy use, bandwidth savings, or privacy guarantees.

In the envisioned deployment, Stage 1 (EfficientNet-B5 with mixed-precision quantization on edge runtimes such as OpenVINO/NCNN, following edge intelligence principles \citep{edge_intelligence, edge_its_survey}) would run per camera and emit only structured count payloads, while Stage 2 (TA-STGCN) would execute centrally on the aggregated counts. Transmitting a two-element count vector instead of a $\sim$500~KB 1080p snapshot gives an illustrative payload-size ratio of $\sim$$62{,}500\times$, though this excludes protocol overhead, retransmissions, and device measurements and is not an observed bandwidth result. Validating the architecture requires end-to-end measurement across 608 devices, which we leave to future work.

\subsection{Parameter Economy vs. GPU Activation Memory}

A key hardware design insight from our benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds}) is that static parameter count ($N_{\text{params}}$) alone is insufficient to evaluate model efficiency for city-scale graph architectures. While SOTA models like STAEformer maintain moderate parameter footprints ($\sim 568\text{K}$), their internal dynamic soft-attention allocations generate large activation footprints across $N=608$ nodes (\textbf{11,509.1 MB} peak GPU memory). The anomalously low footprint of iTransformer (265.2 MB) reflects its design: as an Inverted Transformer, it applies self-attention over individual variates/nodes rather than materializing an $N \times N$ spatial attention map, so it incurs no graph-scale activation memory---which is precisely why it is not a graph-structured baseline and is treated separately throughout our analysis.

In contrast, TA-STGCN explicitly decouples spatial Chebyshev graph convolutions from temporal self-attention. This raises peak GPU memory over the naive STGCN baseline it extends, yet its economy relative to the heavier baselines---a 25.2\% peak-memory reduction versus STAEformer and a 70.4\% parameter reduction versus ASTGCN (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison})---keeps both axes favorable simultaneously for cost-constrained real-world deployment.

\subsection{Fairness of the Baseline Comparison}
\label{sec:fairness}
We are explicit about three respects in which the Stage~2 comparison is a \emph{controlled implementation comparison} rather than a per-model-optimized contest, because each bears on how the numbers in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds} should be read.

\textbf{Shared loss vs.\ native losses.} Every model is trained under the same Huber objective for experimental control. Some baselines were originally proposed with a different objective (e.g., GraphWaveNet uses an MAE-style loss), and retraining under Huber may not be their individually optimal setting. We accept this trade-off deliberately: a common objective holds the outlier treatment constant and isolates architectural differences, but it means a given baseline's reported number is its Huber-trained result, not its published best. Absolute baseline accuracies should therefore be read as lower-bound estimates of their potential rather than as a ranking of the architectures themselves---and, correspondingly, this is a reason we do \emph{not} headline the small clean-input MAE margins, which sit inside the noise of such loss-choice effects.

\textbf{No per-model hyperparameter search.} We did not run an exhaustive, model-specific hyperparameter search on the 608-node benchmark; baselines use the authors' recommended configurations adapted to the shared input/output dimensions. Individual baselines may therefore be sensitive to further tuning, and their clean-input MAE could improve. This cuts in both directions: the comparison is conservative about declaring any architecture superior on clean inputs, consistent with our effect-size reading (all $|d|<0.1$) that the graph models are practically equivalent in point accuracy.

\textbf{iTransformer as a non-graph baseline.} iTransformer is included as a strong multivariate-time-series reference, but it is not a graph-structured forecaster (Section~\ref{sec:deployment_arch}). We therefore treat it separately throughout: it is excluded from ``graph-baseline'' statements, and where it is the closest competitor under noise (Level~4) we report the gap as modest and non-decisive rather than folding it into a graph-vs-graph claim. Its value in the table is to show that a non-graph attention model can also be noise-robust, which bounds how much of TA-STGCN's robustness is attributable to graph structure specifically versus to temporal attention in general.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations remain in this study.

\textbf{First}, the Stage 2 models are trained on vehicle count pseudo-labels produced by Stage 1, so forecasting fidelity is inherently bounded by visual perception error margins.

\textbf{Second}, the total system computational cost is substantial. Although the Stage 2 graph model is lightweight (453K parameters), Stage 1 requires deploying a 28.3M-parameter EfficientNet-B5 per camera---across a 608-node network, 608 independent deployments of the same model (over $17$ billion parameters in aggregate), posing significant edge-deployment, update, latency, and energy challenges. MobileNetV3-Large (4.2M parameters) offers a smaller alternative at a higher Stage 1 MAE ($4.0126$ versus $3.6363$); quantization, distillation, and device-level throughput measurements are required before choosing a deployment backbone. Relatedly, the edge runtimes of Section~\ref{sec:deployment_arch} remain conceptual, as the reported experiments retrieve images centrally.

\textbf{Third}, the Stage 1 benchmark compares only classification backbones under a direct-regression head and does not include specialized dense-counting methods (density-map estimators such as MCNN \citep{mcnn}, CSRNet \citep{csrnet}, and CAN \citep{can}; point-supervised counters such as P2PNet and DM-Count; or tracking-by-detection pipelines such as YOLO + ByteTrack \citep{yolo}). We position direct regression as a \emph{practical trade-off} for this dataset rather than a claim of superiority: the aggregate count labels used here avoid the per-vehicle point/density annotation and per-camera calibration cost that such methods require, but a head-to-head comparison on matched point annotations remains open, alongside semi-supervised learning and additional annotation to address the 5,012-image dataset ceiling.

\textbf{Fourth}, the evaluation is constrained to a single city and a 3-month temporal window, without extreme seasonal or weather variation.

\textbf{Fifth}, the Stage 2 benchmark covers a representative set of graph and Transformer baselines up to 2024 but omits the most recent 2024--2025 architectures: spatio-temporal state-space models (Mamba \citep{mamba}), diffusion-based forecasters (e.g., CSDI, DiffSTG), and patch-based or large-language-model time-series methods (e.g., TimeXer, PatchTST \citep{patchtst}, Crossformer). These families are not yet standardized for camera-sensed, category-preserving graph inputs and would require substantial re-engineering and per-model tuning on our 608-node benchmark; a direct comparison remains an important direction for future work.

\textbf{Finally}, the static RBF graph cannot adapt to non-physical dependencies or incident-driven structural shifts; adaptive graph learning and sensitivity tests for graph parameters are important future comparisons. The noise-robustness analysis also uses a single (Gamma) reference distribution---a sensitivity ablation under matched Weibull and log-normal samplers, and per-baseline retraining under native losses, would further harden the comparison.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced a two-stage framework for urban traffic monitoring and multi-horizon forecasting, evaluated on a real-world 608-node camera network in Ho Chi Minh City. The experimental pipeline combines camera-based vehicle perception (EfficientNet-B5) and graph forecasting (TA-STGCN); an edge-cloud realization is proposed for future deployment rather than implemented in the reported experiments.

Comprehensive evaluations show that TA-STGCN reaches competitive point-forecasting accuracy ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2401}$) with low run-to-run variation ($\text{CV}=0.33\%$); on clean inputs the evaluated graph models are practically equivalent in point accuracy. Under simulated perception noise, the robustness advantage \emph{stratifies by competitor class}: TA-STGCN degrades far more gracefully than the non-attention graph/RNN baselines (Cohen's $d$ up to $+5.84$ vs.\ GraphWaveNet), while against the attention-based baselines (STAEformer, iTransformer) its margin is only small-to-medium under extreme noise ($d \in [0.59, 0.67]$ at Level 4) and not a decisive separation---i.e., the robustness is a property of the attention-equipped design class on this benchmark, not a dominance of TA-STGCN over the SOTA attention baselines, with which it is comparably robust while being more parameter-economical (453K parameters, $25.2\%$ less peak training memory than STAEformer). The ablations and noise simulations indicate improved robustness under the evaluated perturbations, while leaving broader claims about optimal attention filtering, graph choice, noise-distribution sensitivity, and deployment performance for future validation. We release this benchmark, dataset, and evaluation protocol to support future work in camera-sensed, motorcycle-dominant traffic forecasting, independent of the specific architectures compared here.

\section*{Ethics and Visual Privacy Statement}
Traffic surveillance camera feeds analyzed in this study were obtained from the public Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal. The experimental collection and inference pipeline retrieved image snapshots centrally; it therefore does not establish an edge-processing or privacy-by-design guarantee. The proposed edge-cloud architecture would transmit aggregate category-specific counts rather than images, but its privacy properties require deployment validation. We do not claim that camera elevation or resolution makes all personally identifiable information impossible to recover. Any public release or redistribution of image-derived data must comply with the Traffic Portal's terms of use, applicable law, and institutional ethics requirements; only materials for which redistribution permission is confirmed should be released.
