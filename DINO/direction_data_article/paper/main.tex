\begin{abstract}
This data article presents \textbf{IC4SD-TrafficSnap}, a multimodal dataset coupling city-scale surveillance camera image time-series (released under CC BY-NC 4.0 for non-commercial academic research) and geospatial road network mapping (licensed under ODbL) across \textbf{608} indexed traffic surveillance stations in the metropolitan urban and peri-urban corridors of Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. The visual corpus contains \textbf{714,123} discrete camera snapshots (\textbf{44.38~GiB} / \textbf{47.66~GB} in standard stream JPEG format) captured over \textbf{93.6 continuous hours} across 5 calendar days (October 2, 2026, 17:37 to October 6, 2026, 15:12 ICT) at an empirical sampling interval of $\mathbf{\Delta T = 269.0 \pm 239.7}$~s (median: $263.0$~s, $p_{90} = 300.0$~s, $p_{95} = 520.0$~s, $p_{99} \approx 1,200.0$~s; nominal target: $300$~s / 5.0 minutes; high variance driven by prolonged link disruptions at 14 peripheral stations). Physical driving distances and directions are extracted from OpenStreetMap using the Open Source Routing Machine (OSRM v5.27.1). From these geospatial road assets, a $608 \times 608$ directed spatial graph topology containing \textbf{2,450} valid corridor links within a \textbf{6.0~km} search cutoff horizon is derived as a physical road connectivity representation, capturing directional asymmetry (\textbf{1,070} unidirectional links without reverse edges and \textbf{232} distance-asymmetric pairs, alongside \textbf{458} metric-symmetric pairs) induced by municipal one-way rules, physical median dividers, and algorithmic corridor pruning. PyTorch data ingestion utilities and dual-transition directed diffusion graph routines (MIT License) are provided. Quantitative optical and privacy audits across the entire \textbf{714,123} visual snapshot corpus confirm zero readable personal data instances ($0.00\%$), as elevated mounting ($>6$~m) and sub-Nyquist frame resolution ($512 \times 288$ px, ground sampling distance $\ge 2.73$~cm/px) render vehicular license plate characters and facial biometrics physically unresolvable, establishing quantified negligible privacy risk.
\end{abstract}

\section{Value of the Data}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heterogeneous Mixed Urban Traffic Representation:} Captures multi-day visual dynamics of a Southeast Asian metropolis where two-wheeled motorized vehicles account for \textbf{70--80\%} of modal traffic volume~\cite{jica2014hcmc}, supporting visual perception and scene decomposition research under dense mutual occlusions and non-lane-based movement patterns.
    \item \textbf{Geospatial Road Network and Derived Directed Graph:} Integrates surveillance visual streams with OpenStreetMap coordinates and shortest-path driving distance matrices ($608 \times 608$, \textbf{2,450} valid corridors). The directed spatial graph serves as a natural derived topological representation of physical road infrastructure connectivity (featuring 1,070 unidirectional links and 232 distance-asymmetric bidirectional pairs), facilitating multi-camera spatio-temporal self-supervised representation learning and network corridor diffusion modeling with forward and backward transition matrices.
    \item \textbf{Roadway Background and Incident Modeling:} The fixed-viewpoint multi-camera time-series across circadian day-night cycles provides an empirical basis for separating static roadway backgrounds from transient vehicular flow, enabling unsupervised anomaly and incident detection.
    \item \textbf{Open Reproducibility and Three-Tier Licensing:} Accompanied by verified PyTorch data loaders, multi-camera batch samplers, and dual-transition directed graph processing routines. The dataset operates under a transparent three-tier licensing framework: visual surveillance imagery is released under \textit{Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0 (CC BY-NC 4.0)} for academic research, the derived road network graph is released under the \textit{Open Database License (ODbL)}, and software utilities are distributed under the \textit{MIT License}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
Large-scale spatial-temporal traffic benchmarks are foundational for developing intelligent transportation algorithms. However, established datasets exhibit two distinct limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sensor-based traffic datasets} (such as METR-LA~\cite{li2018diffusion}, PEMS-BAY~\cite{li2018diffusion}, and LargeST~\cite{liu2023largest}) provide extensive graph topologies and longitudinal 1D time-series, but lack visual context, preventing models from leveraging rich photometric, weather, and vehicle-type features.
    \item \textbf{Computer vision benchmarks} (e.g., UA-DETRAC~\cite{wen2020ua}, CityFlow / AI City Challenge~\cite{tang2019cityflow}, and CityCam~\cite{zhang2017understanding}) provide multi-camera visual imagery but lack city-scale directed road network topologies, and predominantly reflect car-dominant, lane-disciplined traffic in temperate North American or East Asian settings ($960 \times 540$ video clips in Beijing/Tianjin for UA-DETRAC, 40-camera video clips in CityFlow, and low-resolution webcam snapshots in New York City for CityCam).
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{IC4SD-TrafficSnap} addresses these limitations by providing an integrated multimodal dataset: surveillance camera snapshots and geospatial road network mapping across \textbf{608} camera stations in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. From these primary road mapping and spatial assets, a directed spatial graph topology is established as a direct derived representation to support spatial-temporal neural network modeling. A systematic comparison with existing datasets is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_comparison}.

\subsection{Visual Snapshot Modality and Temporal Sampling}
The visual corpus consists of discrete 3-channel RGB JPEG snapshots (JFIF 1.01 standard) with a uniform native frame resolution of $\mathbf{512 \times 288}$ pixels (standard stream compression quality factor $\approx 75$--$80$). Each image file adheres to a deterministic identifier schema:
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{\{station\_id\}\_\{unix\_timestamp\}.jpg}
\end{equation}
where $\texttt{station\_id}$ indexes the surveillance camera station, and $\texttt{unix\_timestamp}$ represents UTC epoch seconds (Vietnam Standard Time is $\text{UTC} + 7$). Exactly \textbf{608 camera stations} are indexed in the dataset across the municipal road network.

Snapshots were acquired across \textbf{93.6 continuous hours} spanning 5 calendar days (from October 2, 2026, 17:37 ICT to October 6, 2026, 15:12 ICT). The empirical inter-snapshot acquisition interval averages $\mathbf{\Delta T = 269.0 \pm 239.7}$~s (median: $263.0$~s, $p_{90} = 300.0$~s, $p_{95} = 520.0$~s, $p_{99} \approx 1,200.0$~s, maximum: $4.12$~hours; nominal target: $300$~s / 5.0 minutes). The standard deviation arises from practical IoT network conditions and upstream refresh schedules: the municipal streaming server updates its internal frame buffer at intervals of approximately $240$--$270$~s, and client asynchronous polling with cache-busting headers retrieves the newly available frame as soon as rendered, explaining why the median interval ($263.0$~s) is faster than the nominal $300$~s target. The heavy right tail is driven by prolonged cellular/fiber link disruptions or scheduled municipal power maintenance at 14 peripheral stations (which recorded $<600$ snapshots). In contrast, the core municipal network (512 stations, $84.2\%$) operated with over $95\%$ continuous temporal coverage. The reported acquisition pass rate of $99.85\%$ reflects HTTP ingestion reliability (i.e., only $0.15\%$ of incoming responses were corrupt payloads or server placeholder cards), while offline transmission outages produce discrete missing intervals. An empirical analysis of inter-snapshot spacing indicates that $\mathbf{88.4\%}$ of intervals satisfy $\Delta T \le 300$~s, $\mathbf{7.8\%}$ fall between $300$ and $600$~s, and only $\mathbf{3.8\%}$ exceed $600$~s. For spatio-temporal deep learning architectures that require regular temporal grids (\path{code/camera_sampler.py}), snapshots are quantized onto fixed discrete 5-minute intervals ($\tau = 300$~s) via nearest-neighbor binning when the alignment deviation is bounded within $\pm 2.5$~minutes ($\le 150$~s); for larger gaps exceeding $300$~s ($11.6\%$ of intervals), temporal missing masks (`mask = 0`) are assigned rather than duplicating stale frames.

The total archive contains \textbf{714,123 valid surveillance snapshots} (\textbf{44.38~GiB} binary volume, equivalent to \textbf{47.66~GB} decimal). Across the \textbf{608 camera stations}, the dataset delivers a dense, multi-camera visual corpus averaging $\mathbf{1,174.5 \pm 235.1}$ snapshots per station (theoretical nominal target for 93.6~hours at $300$~s polling is $\approx 1,123$ frames, with empirical peak operational stations reaching up to $1,268$ frames due to the $240$--$270$~s buffer refresh cycle). The variation in snapshot counts per station stems from real-world municipal IoT telecommunications infrastructure: peripheral stations occasionally experienced temporary cellular/fiber link disruptions or scheduled municipal power maintenance. Quantitatively, \textbf{512 stations} ($84.2\%$) exhibit high operational continuity with $\ge 95\%$ temporal coverage ($>1,100$ snapshots); \textbf{82 stations} ($13.5\%$) provide moderate coverage ($600$--$1,100$ snapshots); and only \textbf{14 stations} ($2.3\%$) provide $<50\%$ coverage ($<600$ snapshots) due to prolonged peripheral transmission outages. All 608 stations remain geodetically cataloged and topologically mapped in the accompanying spatial graph.

Temporal partitioning based on standard clock hours (Daytime: 06:00--18:00 ICT; Nighttime: 18:00--06:00 ICT) yields \textbf{348,464 daytime frames} ($48.8\%$) and \textbf{365,659 nighttime frames} ($51.2\%$). Because data collection commenced late in the afternoon (17:37) on Day 1 and concluded mid-afternoon (15:12) on Day 5, afternoon time slots span 5 calendar days while late-night intervals span 4 cycles. An empirical audit of on-screen overlay timestamps across 500 sampled daylight frames indicates that client ingestion introduces an average offset of $\mathbf{\Delta t_{\text{lag}} = 15.0 \pm 4.2}$~s relative to camera hardware clocks on NTP-synchronized stations. This reflects the elapsed duration between frame rendering by the municipal HLS streaming transcoder and client socket reception. Crucially, the audit revealed that approximately $1.8\%$ of camera endpoints (11 stations) exhibit de-synchronized internal hardware clocks (displaying historical factory default times due to lack of local NTP synchronization). Consequently, the client-recorded UTC epoch timestamp embedded in each file name serves as the canonical, synchronized temporal index. The spatial distribution of camera stations and diurnal photometric properties are presented in Figures~\ref{fig:spatial_map} and \ref{fig:temporal_photometric}. A quantitative breakdown of the visual snapshot corpus is provided in Table~\ref{tab:summary_stats}.

\subsection{Geospatial Road Network and Derived Directed Spatial Graph Topology}
The structural substrate of the dataset is defined by the physical roadway network of Ho Chi Minh City. Comprehensive geospatial metadata is compiled in \path{metadata/routes.csv} (recording station IDs, geographic coordinates, administrative localities, arterial classifications, and camera mounting elevations across all \textbf{608} camera locations) alongside the pairwise shortest driving distance matrix \path{metadata/road_network_distance.csv}.

Rather than treating the spatial graph as an abstract primitive, a directed spatial graph topology is established as a direct \textbf{derived mathematical representation} synthesized from these geospatial road coordinates and OpenStreetMap routing paths. Machine-learning tensors (\path{graph/distance_km.npy}, \path{graph/direction.npy}, and \path{graph/edges.csv}) are structured as $608 \times 608$ arrays for direct ingestion into spatio-temporal graph neural networks. Entry $d_{ij}$ denotes the shortest physical driving distance along navigable OpenStreetMap road links from camera station $i$ to station $j$.

\textbf{Corridor Adjacency Construction Rule:} An unconstrained geometric radius graph within $6.0$~km would yield over $190,000$ links due to dense urban camera spacing in central districts. Instead, the road network graph models sequential arterial corridor connectivity via a rigorous three-stage algorithmic pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Geometric \& Navigability Search:} Pairwise driving routes between all 608 stations are evaluated via OSRM within a spatial bounding cutoff horizon of $\kappa = \mathbf{6.0}$~km;
    \item \textbf{Immediate Downstream Corridor Pruning:} A candidate directed edge $(i, j)$ is retained if and only if station $j$ represents an immediate downstream surveillance node along the roadway without intervening intermediate cameras. An intermediate camera is defined as any third camera $k$ located within an orthogonal buffer of $50$~m from the traversed driving path with $d_{ik} < d_{ij}$;
    \item \textbf{Directionality Constraints:} Vehicular movement must be legally permissible along the corridor under OpenStreetMap road directionality attributes (\texttt{oneway} tags).
\end{enumerate}
Diagonal entries ($i = j$) denote regularized self-distances ($d_{ii} = 0.0$ in numeric arrays), and non-connected pairs are represented by $\text{NaN}$.

Under this three-stage sequential corridor adjacency rule, the derived road graph contains exactly \textbf{2,450 valid directed edges} across \textbf{1,760 connected node pairs} (network sparsity: \textbf{99.34\%}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unidirectional Corridor Links (No Direct Reverse Edge):} \textbf{1,070 node pairs} ($60.80\%$ of connected topology) possess directed connectivity in only one travel direction. This structural asymmetry arises from two distinct mechanisms: (i) designated one-way municipal traffic regulations on arterial pairs (verified against OpenStreetMap \texttt{oneway=yes} tags); and (ii) algorithmic corridor pruning asymmetry, wherein the reverse travel path between $j$ and $i$ passes through intermediate junction cameras located along the return carriageway, thereby decomposing the return route into multiple shorter links and leaving no direct $(j, i)$ single-hop corridor link.
    \item \textbf{Bidirectional Corridor Pairs:} \textbf{690 node pairs} ($39.20\%$ of connected topology) are mutually accessible in both traffic directions within $6.0$~km.
    \item \textbf{Distance Asymmetry:} Among the 690 bidirectional pairs, \textbf{458 pairs} ($66.38\%$) exhibit metric symmetry ($|d_{ij} - d_{ji}| < 50$~m), whereas \textbf{232 pairs} ($33.62\%$) exhibit significant distance asymmetry ($|d_{ij} - d_{ji}| \ge 50$~m), directly induced by physical median barriers, grade-separated flyovers, and compulsory U-turn routing on divided dual-carriageway boulevards. The mean directional discrepancy across bidirectional corridors is $\mathbf{115.9 \pm 266.2}$~m (median: $24.0$~m), reaching a maximum divergence of $\mathbf{2,780.0}$~m ($2.78$~km).
    \item \textbf{Topological Node Roles and Connectivity:} Structural degree analysis identifies \textbf{598} regular interconnected stations, \textbf{5} sink stations (in-degree $>0$, out-degree $=0$: Stations 212, 284, 443, 446, 516), \textbf{3} source stations (in-degree $=0$, out-degree $>0$: Stations 215, 498, 557), and \textbf{2} peripheral stations situated beyond $6.0$~km from all others (in-degree $=0$, out-degree $=0$: Stations 141, 489). The graph contains \textbf{6 weakly connected components} (dominated by a giant component of 599 nodes, 98.5\% of the network, alongside five small components of sizes 3, 2, 2, 1, and 1) and \textbf{19 strongly connected components}. Valid edge routing distances span from $3.0$~m to $6,000.0$~m (mean: $1,159.6 \pm 1,088.9$~m; median: $823.0$~m; interquartile range: $[384.0, 1,560.0]$~m). The minimal routing distances (e.g., $3.0$~m) represent co-located directional surveillance cameras mounted on the same physical gantry or mast monitoring orthogonal approach directions (e.g., North-South vs. East-West traffic approaches) at complex multi-way intersections; these capture local intersection queue interactions rather than sequential downstream highway corridors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Design, Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Surveillance Infrastructure and Asynchronous Ingestion Pipeline}
The visual data source is the public municipal traffic surveillance web portal maintained by the Ho Chi Minh City Department of Transportation (\url{https://giaothong.hochiminhcity.gov.vn}). Cameras are mounted on elevated steel gantries and traffic signal masts at heights between $\mathbf{6.0}$ and $\mathbf{15.0}$~meters above street level (as cataloged in the \texttt{camera\_elevation\_m} attribute of \path{routes.csv}), oriented at downward oblique angles ($15^\circ - 40^\circ$) to monitor arterial queues and intersection approaches.

To ingest snapshots across \textbf{608 concurrent camera endpoints} without inducing network bottlenecks or file corruption, an asynchronous Python ingestion pipeline was deployed:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{HTTP/1.1 Connection Pooling:} Persistent sessions with Keep-Alive connection pooling and automatic exponential backoff retries (maximum $3$ retries, backoff factor $0.3$) managed through \texttt{urllib3}.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Offset Ingestion Timer:} Thread execution latency is tracked during each cycle to adjust polling offsets, targeting nominal $300$~s inter-snapshot spacing across the fleet.
    \item \textbf{Atomic File Serialization:} Retrieved JPEG streams are decoded in memory (\texttt{cv2.imdecode}) and verified before being written to temporary files and committed via atomic OS renames (\texttt{os.replace}), preventing truncated or zero-byte files on disk.
    \item \textbf{Automated Artifact Removal:} Municipal transmission interruptions occasionally yield standardized error placeholder cards ($284 \times 177$ pixels, payload size $5.8$--$7.2$~KB). The ingestion pipeline automatically identifies and discards these placeholder cards, ensuring valid traffic snapshots in the released archive (overall acquisition pass rate: \textbf{99.85\%}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Road Network Distance Extraction from OpenStreetMap}
Physical routing distances $d_{ij}$ and driving trajectories were computed using the Open Source Routing Machine (\textbf{OSRM v5.27.1}) engine operating on OpenStreetMap planet road network data extracted in \textbf{October 2026} (\copyright\ OpenStreetMap contributors, licensed under the Open Database License ODbL). Exact geographic coordinates (latitude, longitude) cataloged in \path{metadata/routes.csv} were mapped to the nearest navigable road centerline using OSRM's nearest-node projection, with a radial snapping error under $15.0$~meters across all stations. 

Because surveillance camera endpoints are predominantly deployed along primary municipal arterials, boulevards, and bridge approaches rather than narrow residential alleys, routing was calculated using the standardized motor-vehicle driving profile. This profile accurately captures urban traffic flow rules, median divider turn restrictions, grade-separated overpasses, and designated one-way circulation patterns across the metropolitan core. While mixed motorcycle traffic constitutes a dominant volume fraction in Ho Chi Minh City, both vehicular modes share identical directional corridors along primary arterials; informal motorcycle cut-throughs in residential alleyways fall outside the macroscopic coverage of the municipal camera network. Pairwise shortest driving routes were queried within a spatial bounding search horizon of $\kappa = \mathbf{6.0}$~km, from which the three-stage corridor pipeline extracted exactly \textbf{2,450 valid directed corridors}.

\subsection{Directed Graph Modeling Formulations}
To facilitate spatio-temporal graph learning on this directed asymmetric topology, two standard mathematical formulations are provided in \path{code/graph_utils.py}:

\subsubsection{Directed Dual-Transition Matrices (DCRNN / Graph WaveNet Formulation)}
For directed road graphs, modeling traffic propagation requires preserving directional asymmetry. Following the diffusion convolution framework of Li et al.~\cite{li2018diffusion}, the weighted adjacency matrix $W_{ij}$ is computed using a thresholded Gaussian Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel evaluated on the valid directed corridor links:
\begin{equation}
    W_{ij} = \begin{cases} 
    \exp\left( -\frac{d_{ij}^2}{\sigma^2} \right), & \text{if } (i, j) \in E \\ 
    0, & \text{if } (i, j) \notin E 
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $E$ denotes the set of \textbf{2,450 valid directed edges}, and $\sigma = \mathbf{1.09}$~km is the empirical standard deviation of valid road distances. Computing $W$ strictly over pruned sequential corridors $E$ avoids spurious dense cross-connectivity while preserving physical routing topology. Under this formulation with $\sigma = 1.09$~km, proximal camera stations with $d_{ij} \le 1.0$~km retain significant connection weight ($W_{ij} \ge \exp(-(1.0/1.09)^2) \approx 0.43$), attenuating to $\approx 0.15$ at $1.5$~km and decaying toward negligible levels at larger separations ($\approx 0.005$ at $2.5$~km). The distance threshold $\kappa = 6.0$~km serves as the initial geometric search envelope for corridor path discovery rather than an RBF truncation cutoff. Directed random walk diffusion is governed by a forward transition matrix $P_f$ (downstream traffic flow) and a backward transition matrix $P_b$ (upstream sensory influence):
\begin{equation}
    P_f = D_O^{-1} W, \quad P_b = D_I^{-1} W^\top
\end{equation}
where $D_O = \text{diag}(\sum_{j} W_{ij})$ and $D_I = \text{diag}(\sum_{i} W_{ij})$ denote the out-degree and in-degree diagonal matrices, respectively. To handle sink, source, or isolated nodes ($D_{O, ii} = 0$ or $D_{I, ii} = 0$), regularized self-loops ($\tilde{W} = W + I_N$) or zero-degree masked normalizations are implemented in \path{code/graph_utils.py}, ensuring proper row-stochastic diffusion matrices.

\subsubsection{Symmetrized Normalized Laplacian (ChebNet / STGCN Formulation)}
For spectral graph convolutions that require a self-adjoint operator (e.g., ChebNet~\cite{defferrard2016convolutional}, STGCN~\cite{yu2018spatio}), the adjacency matrix is symmetrized as $W_{\text{sym}} = \frac{1}{2}(W + W^\top)$. The symmetric normalized Laplacian $L_{\text{sym}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is computed as~\cite{kipf2017semi}:
\begin{equation}
    L_{\text{sym}} = I_N - D_{\text{sym}}^{-1/2} W_{\text{sym}} D_{\text{sym}}^{-1/2}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\text{sym}} = \text{diag}(\sum_{j} W_{\text{sym}, ij})$. By spectral graph theory, the largest eigenvalue of the normalized Laplacian satisfies $\lambda_{\max} \le 2.0$, and the scaled Chebyshev operator is $\tilde{L} = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} L_{\text{sym}} - I_N$. Users should note that symmetrization intentionally eliminates directional flow asymmetry, which is critical for modeling asymmetric urban arterial dynamics.

\subsection{Empirical Image Quality and Photometric Audit}
Image quality descriptors were computed across the dataset to evaluate photometric diversity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Perceived Luminance ($Y$):} Calculated via standard ITU-R BT.601 conversion $Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B$. The empirical overall mean luminance is $\mathbf{98.23 \pm 16.35}$ (range: $[66.4, 126.8]$, Figure~\ref{fig:temporal_photometric}a). Nighttime luminance remains centered between $\mathbf{87}$ and $\mathbf{90}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal_photometric}a) due to dense municipal LED and high-pressure sodium street lighting along urban arterials, combined with the automatic gain control (AGC) and exposure compensation of municipal cameras.
    \item \textbf{Root-Mean-Square (RMS) Contrast:} Evaluated as the pixel standard deviation across viewports, yielding $\mathbf{45.70}$, confirming substantial textural variation between asphalt roadways and vehicle clusters.
    \item \textbf{Shannon Spatial Entropy:} Histogram pixel entropy averages $\mathbf{7.28 \pm 0.33}$~bits (theoretical upper bound: $8.0$~bits), confirming high spatial information density without saturation clipping.
    \item \textbf{Laplacian Edge Sharpness:} Grayscale Laplacian variance $\text{Var}(\nabla^2 I)$ averages $\mathbf{3,015.71 \pm 1,499.86}$. An empirical reference baseline of $\text{Var}(\nabla^2 I) \ge 50$ was adopted as an engineering heuristic to flag severe optical defocus or stream degradation~\cite{pech2000diatom}. The substantial empirical mean of $\mathbf{3,015.71 \pm 1,499.86}$ reflects high scene detail from rich urban textures (vehicular clusters, road markings, roadside foliage) as well as standard JPEG compression block boundaries, confirming that camera viewports maintain usable focus.
    \item \textbf{Inter-Snapshot Dynamics:} Successive-frame pixel difference analysis across consecutive 5-minute sampling pairs was evaluated on 8-bit grayscale channels in floating-point precision ($\text{diff} = |I_t - I_{t-1}|$). The empirical Mean Absolute Difference (MAD) yields a median of $\mathbf{9.45}$ ($\mu = 10.82 \pm 3.65$, range: $[2.50, 32.40]$, interquartile range: $[7.20, 13.80]$), with an active pixel displacement ratio yielding a median of $\mathbf{15.82\%}$ ($\mu = 18.41 \pm 6.20\%$ of frame pixels satisfying $|I_t - I_{t-1}| > 15$). This moderate pixel variation corresponds directly to vehicular displacement and queue progression across traffic lanes, while static structural regions (buildings, sidewalks, road surfaces) remain invariant. During data ingestion, identical stream buffer frames ($\text{MAD} < 0.5$) resulting from upstream rendering latency were programmatically screened out. Across the archived operational time-series, the empirical 1st percentile of consecutive MAD is $\mathbf{2.80}$ gray levels (strictly within the $[2.50, 32.40]$ empirical distribution and well above the $0.5$ deduplication threshold), confirming continuous vehicular and environmental variations without static playback freezing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Visual Privacy and Empirical PII Audit}
To evaluate alignment with personal data protection regulations in Vietnam (Decree 13/2023/ND-CP and Law 91/2025/QH15), a multi-tiered empirical privacy audit was conducted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Census Audit:} An automated optical and privacy audit across the entire corpus of \textbf{714,123 visual snapshots} was conducted to evaluate compliance and personal data exposure risks.
    \item \textbf{Optical Perspective and Ground Sampling Distance (GSD):} Cameras are mounted at elevations of $6.0$--$15.0$~m with downward oblique pitch angles ($15^\circ - 40^\circ$). Under native $512 \times 288$ frame resolution, the nominal GSD across mid-road viewports ($25$--$35$~m observation distance) ranges from $\mathbf{2.73}$ to $\mathbf{3.25}$~cm/pixel, degrading to $>5.0$~cm/pixel in the distant background ($>40$~m).
    \item \textbf{License Plate Legibility Limits:} Standard Vietnamese motorcycle license plates measure $19.0 \times 14.0$~cm. At nominal GSD, plates occupy approximately $7 \times 5$ pixels, with individual alphanumeric characters subtending under $2$ pixels in stroke height. Even in the closest foreground lanes, vehicle motion blur and lossy JPEG compression prevent alphanumeric characters from satisfying the optical Nyquist-Shannon sampling limit required for legibility (which requires $\ge 16$ pixels of character stroke height for reliable optical character recognition).
    \item \textbf{Facial Anonymity \& Physical Occlusion:} Motorcyclists in Vietnam commonly wear safety helmets and protective fabric face coverings (masks worn against tropical solar radiation and road dust). Combined with elevated steep perspective, facial biometric features project onto fewer than $8 \times 8$ pixels, rendering individual human identities unresolvable.
    \item \textbf{Foreground Risk Analysis and Ground-Truth Audit:} Automated biometric and license plate detectors returned zero legible detections across the full dataset ($0.00\%$). While automated detectors inherently exhibit reduced recall on low-resolution imagery below the Nyquist limit, physical optical geometry provides a structural privacy barrier. Standard Vietnamese motorcycle plates ($19.0 \times 14.0$~cm) project to at most $7 \times 5$ pixels at nominal mid-road distances, with character stroke height under $2$ pixels. Even in foreground lanes ($<15$~m) where ground sampling distance reaches $2.73$~cm/pixel, steep downward pitch angles ($15^\circ - 40^\circ$), plate inclination relative to the optical axis, motion blur from moving vehicles, and lossy JPEG compression ensure that alphanumeric characters remain below the $\ge 16$ pixel stroke height threshold required for automated optical character recognition. Independent manual inspection on a targeted control sample of 1,000 daylight frames from the lowest-mounted cameras ($H \approx 6.0$~m) during peak lighting confirmed zero legible characters and zero identifiable facial biometrics, establishing quantified negligible privacy risk.
    \item \textbf{Contextual Privacy Scope:} Beyond biometric faces and vehicle plates, urban contextual features such as pedestrian silhouettes, commercial storefront signs, and distinctive commercial vehicles are occasionally visible; however, given the macroscopic elevated field of view and discontinuous 5-minute sampling intervals, the dataset does not permit continuous individual tracking or trajectory reconstruction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Observation Time Window:} The \textbf{93.6-hour} continuous observation window captures multi-day diurnal transitions across weekdays and weekends across both the historical metropolitan core and peri-urban expansion districts (Thu Duc City, District 12, Binh Chanh, Hoc Mon, up to $10.99^\circ\text{N}$), but does not encompass longitudinal multi-month seasonal cycles (monsoon wet vs. dry season).
    \item \textbf{Snapshot Sampling vs. Continuous Video:} With a 5-minute nominal polling interval ($\Delta T = 269.0 \pm 239.7$~s), the dataset is designed for macroscopic traffic state analysis, self-supervised pre-training, and spatial graph modeling, but does not support continuous trajectory tracking or optical flow.
    \item \textbf{Surveillance Web Stream Resolution:} Images are serialized at $\mathbf{512 \times 288}$ JPEG compression as delivered by the public municipal web portal, reflecting real-world public IoT camera stream conditions rather than raw uncompressed sensor feeds.
    \item \textbf{Unlabeled Bulk Corpus:} The 714,123-frame corpus is released unlabeled to facilitate self-supervised pre-training, spatio-temporal graph representation learning, and roadway background extraction without manual annotation biases.
    \item \textbf{Urban Arterial Focus and Modal Generalization:} Camera stations are stationed exclusively on major urban arterials, avenues, and bridge approaches. While these corridors capture the predominant flows of both motorcycles (70--80\%) and four-wheeled motor vehicles, the dataset does not encompass narrow residential alley networks (where two-wheelers frequently navigate informal cut-throughs).
    \item \textbf{Physical Infrastructure vs. Dynamic Flow Topology:} The derived directed spatial graph models navigable roadway infrastructure connectivity and distance relationships; it reflects static physical road geometry rather than dynamic, time-varying traffic flow propagation or congestion states.
\end{enumerate}
